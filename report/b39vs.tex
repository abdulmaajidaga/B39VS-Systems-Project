\begin{abstract}
  This paper presents the design and evaluation of Hazmat, an autonomous mobile manipulator developed for indoor hazardous waste handling. The system combines a mecanum-wheeled base for omni-directional mobility, a 3-DOF robotic arm with a four-bar linkage for object manipulation, and a stereo vision system with LiDAR for perception. Navigation is achieved using Cartographer SLAM with encoder and IMU-based odometry. A micro-ROS architecture enables real-time communication between a Raspberry Pi 5 and an ESP32-S3, facilitating distributed control. Design iterations were guided by CAD modeling and Unity simulation. Experimental validation in controlled environments demonstrated reliable localization, navigation, and waste manipulation, highlighting the system's potential for deployment in industrial safety applications.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Project overview}

The project involves the design and development of an autonomous mobile manipulator capable of picking up waste from waste points and disposing of it at the base station. 

The robot, designated 'Hazmat' manufactured by SLAM Industries, is equipped with a Raspberry Pi 5 as its main processor, assisted by an ESP32-S3 to offload computational tasks. This configuration reduces the processing burden on the Pi and provides an additional safeguard against potential hardware faults due to faulty wiring. 

Hazmat is fitted with mecanum wheels, enabling omni-directional movement and allowing it to smoothly traverse through tight spaces within a factory environment. The robot utilizes a stereo-depth camera (Oak-D Pro W) with an integrated IMU, motor encoders, and a 2D LiDAR (RpLiDAR A3) to perform simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), enabling it to autonomously locate waste containers and navigate to disposal points. 

Hazmat is also equipped with a 3-DOF manipulator designed using a four-bar linkage mechanism, and a dual-claw soft servo gripper for efficient waste pickup and placement. The servo motors were modified to output potentiometer feedback to the Pi, enabling real-time inverse kinematics calculations for the robotic arm. Power is supplied by a lithium polymer battery that drives the wheels, while a buck converter is used to step down voltage for the robot's electronic systems.

\section{Background and Theory}

\subsection{Literature review}

\subsubsection{Mobile base}

\begin{itemize}
  
  \item Advanced Autonomous Platforms: Some systems employ model-based motion control to achieve precise navigation. For example, a four-wheel-drive mobile robot with wheel encoders was given a tuned PI controller for path tracking, yielding a tracking error under 1.5\% in experiments \ref{kaliDevelopmentControlMobile2024}. Another design integrates multiple sensors (light, touch, ultrasonic) and utilizes a trapezoidal acceleration-deceleration profile with PID control for smooth, accurate motion [2]. This enabled reliable line-following (e.g. along an S-curve track) and real-time obstacle avoidance on the mobile base.
  \item Teleoperated Bases: Simpler mobile bases often forego autonomous navigation. One wireless robotic arm platform based on Arduino Mega is driven via a PS2 controller, moving forward/reverse and turning in discrete steps [3]. Its performance (speed over distance) was characterized under different battery voltages, showing that insufficient voltage (below ~7V) dramatically slows or even halts the robot [3]. Similarly, a bomb-disposal robot employs an RF remote control and a microcontroller to drive a wheeled base [4]. An onboard camera provides the human operator with feedback as the robot moves in all directions and even free-runs; the base could carry a gripped object up to ~1kg [4]. These teleoperated designs are straightforward but rely entirely on human guidance for navigation.

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Manipulator}

\begin{itemize}
  
  \item Integrated Arms for Pick-and-Place: Many mobile manipulators incorporate an onboard robotic arm for interacting with objects. One platform uses a 4-DOF arm mounted on a NI sbRIO-controlled base to capture selected objects identified by the vision system and place them at target locations [2]. The arm's joints are driven by DC servomotors, and a PID control law coordinates the arm motion for accuracy. Experimental tests showed the arm moving in multiple axes (up/down, forward/back) to reach and grasp the object once the base was in position [2]. Another system features a 3-DOF manipulator (plus gripper) on a mobile platform, where the end-effector trajectory is planned using inverse kinematics\ref{kaliDevelopmentControlMobile2024}. After the base performs path tracking to the work site, the manipulator's position kinematics (with a shoulder offset) are solved to execute the pick-and-place task precisely.
  \item Servo-Based Arms and Payload Limits: In a more minimalist design, a wireless mobile robot arm built on Arduino uses a 4-DOF revolute servo arm for basic pick-and-place [3]. While effective for light objects, its payload is limited - tests showed it can lift 50-150 g, but at ~200 g the servos could no longer function properly [3]. By contrast, a remotely operated EOD robot implemented a 3-DOF arm capable of gripping objects up to 1 kg weight [4]. This arm, controlled via a PIC microcontroller, was observed through a camera and maneuverer by an operator to safely dispose of dangerous items [4]. The lower degree of freedom and direct remote control simplified the arm's design while still accomplishing the essential task.

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping)}

\begin{itemize}
  
  \item Structured Navigation vs. SLAM: Some mobile robots avoid full SLAM by using structured environment cues. For instance, one system notes the difficulty of rebuilding an unknown map in real-world complexity and instead focuses on efficient motion planning [2]. It uses line-following (via IR sensors) and known landmarks to navigate to targets, trading autonomy for simplicity. This approach works well in constrained or known environments but doesn't generalize beyond prepared trajectories.
  \item 2D LIDAR SLAM Techniques: For robots that do explore unknown areas, 2D LIDAR-based SLAM algorithms are common. A direct comparison of popular methods found that GMapping (particle-filter grid mapping) sometimes failed to produce a consistent map, whereas Hector SLAM and Google Cartographer yielded accurate maps that matched ground-truth trajectories within a few centimeters [5]. In fact, Cartographer's real-time loop closure approach (using scan-to-submap matching) enabled large indoor areas to be mapped (tens of thousands of m²) with high fidelity on limited computing hardware [6]. These laser SLAM systems publish maps and pose estimates that can guide navigation for a mobile base.
  \item Vision-Based SLAM: Monocular visual SLAM has also been explored, though it poses challenges in practice. Tests with ROS-based monocular SLAM on a mobile robot showed issues like scale ambiguity (no inherent distance scale) and heavy computation requirements - the processing had to be offloaded to an external PC, and manual adjustments were needed to fix scale. This indicates that without depth sensors, additional measures are required for reliable vision-only SLAM. Other feature-based approaches use environmental features to aid SLAM; for example, an EKF-SLAM algorithm representing walls and corridors as line segments was validated in a real indoor environment [7]. By extracting line features from laser scans and updating their parameters in the filter, the robot simultaneously localized itself and built a compact line-based map.
  \item No-SLAM Teleoperation: It's worth noting that teleoperated systems dispense with SLAM entirely. Robots under direct remote control rely on the operator's situational awareness (often via on-board video feed) for navigation [4]. This eliminates the need for onboard mapping or localization algorithms, simplifying the design at the cost of requiring constant human input and a reliable communication link.

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Single Board Computer}

\begin{itemize}
  
  \item Embedded Controller Platforms: Advanced mobile manipulators often use a dedicated single-board computer or embedded controller to handle all the subsystems. In one design, a National Instruments sbRIO-9606 (branded as a KNR controller) serves as the brain of the robot[2]. This real-time embedded platform integrates sensor I/O, motor drivers, and even vision processing capabilities, allowing the robot to process camera images and control motors in a closed-loop without a separate PC. The KNR controller operates as an independent system, reading encoder feedback and running algorithms (like Otsu thresholding and template matching for object tracking) on-board. Such an SBC provides robust performance and timing for coordination of the mobile base and arm.
  \item Microcontroller-Only Designs: In contrast, some robots are built without a traditional SBC by leveraging microcontroller boards. The wireless mobile robotic arm, for example, is driven purely by an Arduino Mega 2560 microcontroller [3]. The Arduino interfaces directly with the wireless receiver and outputs control signals to motors, effectively acting as both the high-level and low-level controller. This greatly simplifies the architecture and reduces cost/power, but it means the system has limited computing power — it cannot easily perform heavy tasks like vision processing or SLAM on-board. Likewise, the bomb-disposal robot relies on a PIC microcontroller for all decision-making [4], indicating no onboard OS or computer beyond the microcontroller; the video transmission is handled by a separate analog/digital link to the operator, not by an onboard computer. These designs show that an SBC isn't always necessary when the task scope is limited.
  \item Offloading Computation: When higher processing capability is needed, designers may incorporate a more powerful SBC or offload computation to an external system. In one SLAM evaluation, for instance, the robot's sensor data was sent to a laptop for processing because the on-board controller could not handle the intensive monocular SLAM algorithms. This highlights a trade-off: using a capable SBC (like a Raspberry Pi, Jetson, or an FPGA-based board) can enable advanced features on the robot (mapping, complex vision), but it increases power consumption and integration complexity. Each project balances these factors differently depending on requirements.

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Microcontroller}

\begin{itemize}
  
  \item Real-Time Low-Level Control: Microcontrollers play a crucial role in the real-time control of mobile manipulators. They handle tasks such as sensor reading, motor actuation, and ensuring timely responses. In the Arduino-based mobile arm, the ATmega2560 microcontroller generates PWM signals to drive the arm's servos and reads inputs from the wireless game controller. The simplicity of toggling I/O pins at microsecond resolution makes microcontrollers ideal for precise control of motors (e.g., maintaining servo positions or motor speeds). Even in systems with an SBC, microcontroller units (or microcontroller-like cores) may be present for hard real-time tasks. For example, the NI sbRIO's onboard processor and FPGA take on microcontroller duties by interfacing with encoders and sensors directly, closing feedback loops at high speed while the higher-level logic runs in parallel.
  \item Distributed Control Architecture: Some designs use a single microcontroller to manage both the base and manipulator, while others might distribute tasks. In the dangerous-object disposal robot, a single PIC microcontroller (programmed via MPLAB) handles all motion - it parses the decoded remote commands and issues signals to both the drive motors and the arm's servos [4]. This central unit orchestrates wheel movements and gripper actions in sync, proving sufficient for a teleoperated scenario. In more complex robots, one might find multiple microcontrollers (for example, one dedicated to the arm and one to the base) communicating with an SBC. However, increasing the number of controllers requires careful coordination. Overall, microcontrollers are favored for their reliability and deterministic control, especially in safety-critical manipulations, whereas computation-heavy tasks are offloaded to higher-level processors when needed [5].

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Pros and Cons of Different Methods}

\begin{itemize}
  
  \item Autonomy vs. Teleoperation: Fully autonomous mobile manipulators can navigate and perform tasks without human intervention, which is crucial in inaccessible or dynamic environments. They leverage SLAM and path planning to handle unknown areas (e.g., using LIDAR SLAM to map and localize with ~3 cm accuracy [5]). The trade-off is significant system complexity: mapping a real-world environment is challenging [2] and requires robust algorithms and sensor fusion. Teleoperated robots, on the other hand, avoid this complexity by relying on human control. Designs like the PS2-controlled Arduino robot or the RF-controlled bomb disposal unit are far simpler - no SLAM or AI is needed [4]. The downside is they require continuous operator attention and a reliable communications link, and their effectiveness is limited by human reaction time and line-of-sight (or camera view) of the scene. In summary, autonomy increases capability and independence, but teleoperation offers simplicity and ensures a human is in the loop for critical decisions.
  
  \item Control Strategies and Complexity: The choice of control strategy affects both performance and development effort. Advanced control methods (e.g. model-based controllers, inverse kinematics, or even model predictive control) can greatly enhance precision and efficiency. For instance, incorporating kinematic modeling and feedback allowed one mobile base to track paths with only 1.41\% error \ref{kaliDevelopmentControlMobile2024}, and using inverse kinematics enabled accurate arm positioning \ref{kaliDevelopmentControlMobile2024}. However, these methods demand careful system identification, tuning, and computational resources. Simpler control approaches like PID loops or open-loop teleoperation are much easier to implement and require less processing power. They can achieve adequate performance (the NI-based robot effectively followed lines and positioned its arm with basic PID control), but they might yield suboptimal motion (e.g., more overshoot or slower response) compared to optimally tuned model-based controllers. Thus, engineers must balance the need for precision against the available time and hardware for implementing complex controls.
  
  \item Hardware and Integration: Using a high-end integrated controller versus a basic microcontroller board presents a clear trade-off. Integrated SBC solutions (such as the sbRIO or a mini-PC) provide a one-stop platform to handle vision, navigation, and control with ease of integration [2]. They often come with rich development tools (LabVIEW, ROS, etc.) and can run sophisticated algorithms (object recognition, global planning) on-board. The cons are higher cost, greater power consumption, and typically increased weight/size. Conversely, microcontroller-centric builds (Arduino, PIC) are low-cost, lightweight, and energy-efficient [3], but they lack the horsepower for computation-heavy tasks. In practice, some projects augment microcontrollers with external computing (e.g., streaming sensor data to a laptop for processing) or use hybrid setups (microcontroller for real-time control, SBC for higher-level processing). The optimal architecture depends on the application: a research prototype might afford a powerful onboard computer, whereas a field-deployed simple robot might favor minimal electronics for robustness.
  
  \item Actuation and Payload Considerations: Different approaches to actuation lead to different capabilities. Hobby-grade servos (as seen in the Arduino-based arm) are inexpensive and easy to control but impose strict payload limits (~0.15 kg in that design before stalling [3]). They are suitable for educational or light-duty robots but not for heavy manipulation. Upgrading to larger motors or professional servos increases payload (the EOD robot arm handled ~1 kg [4]) and speed but requires more powerful drivers and power supply and often closed-loop control for precision. Moreover, a heavier arm can destabilize a small mobile base when extended; this is why careful mechanical and control design (like lowering speed or using a counterbalance) is needed for heavier payloads. Choosing the right actuators involves balancing the desired payload and reach of the arm against the battery capacity, weight budget, and cost constraints of the overall system.
  
  \item Sensor Suite and Environmental Perception: The methods each system uses to perceive its environment come with pros and cons. A robot equipped with LIDAR and cameras can perform complex tasks like SLAM and object recognition, enabling true autonomy in diverse environments. The NI-based platform, for example, used a camera for vision (with color tracking and template matching) and multiple ultrasonic rangefinders for obstacle detection, giving it a rich awareness of its surroundings. The downside is the integration effort and processing needed for these sensors. Simpler robots that use only basic sensors (bump switches, IR line sensors, a few sonars) are much easier to build and program, but they can only operate in structured settings (following lines, avoiding obstacles directly ahead). If a task demands high reliability in unstructured or dynamic environments, investing in a robust sensor suite is worthwhile; otherwise, a lean sensor setup keeps the system affordable and less complex.
  
  \item Experimental Validation and Results: A key advantage of hardware-focused research is the ability to validate methods in real-world tests. Different approaches have been proven through experiments: the four-wheel mobile base demonstrated its path tracking accuracy on a physical track \ref{kaliDevelopmentControlMobile2024}, and the wireless arm's trials quantified the relationship between battery voltage and speed [3] as well as its payload capacity. SLAM algorithms were not just simulated but also tested on a robot doing laps in an indoor environment, revealing practical differences between mapping methods [5]. Such empirical data is invaluable - it highlights failure modes (e.g., GMapping's map inconsistency [5], or the Arduino arm stalling at 200 g [3]) and verifies that certain methods meet their performance goals (e.g., Cartographer's real-time mapping success [6]). In summary, each method comes with trade-offs, and examining the engineering decisions and outcomes across projects provides guidance for future designs. The best solution for a mobile manipulator will depend on the specific use-case requirements, whether it's the autonomy level, precision, payload, or operating environment that is the top priority.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Motor Control}

\subsubsection{Mecanum wheels}

Mecanum wheels allow a robot to move in any direction without changing its orientation. Each wheel has angled rollers (at 45°) mounted around its rim. When the wheels spin, they create a combination of forward/backward movement and sideways movement. By carefully controlling the speed and direction of each wheel, the robot can move forward, backward, sideways (strafe), diagonally, or rotate on the spot. This gives the robot full omnidirectional mobility, as outlined in Figure \ref{mecanum}. 

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{mecanum.png}
  \caption{A common 4-wheeled mecanum configuration}
  \label{mecanum}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Wheel encoders}

The mecanum wheels come along with an encoder for each wheel and the encoders operate through hall effect sensors. A hall effect sensor works by detecting the magnetic field from a magnet ring attached to the wheel shaft. As the wheel turns, the magnet ring spins with it, causing the magnetic field near the hall sensor to change. Every time the sensor detects a change in the magnetic field, it creates an electrical pulse. These pulses are then sent to the microcontroller to find out the wheel RPM.  

To calculate the RPM, the motor's encoder pulses were counted over a fixed period. Each full revolution generates a known number of pulses (20 pulses per revolution in our motor). The pulse count was divided by the number of pulses per revolution to determine the number of revolutions per second and then multiplied by 60 to convert it into revolutions per minute (RPM). After each calculation, the pulse count was reset for accurate measurement in the next cycle.

\subsubsection{PID control}

A PID controller (Proportional-Integral-Derivative controller) is used in a closed-loop feedback system to control the outer loop of the robot. In this system, the robot's current joint position is continuously measured. The error is calculated by comparing the current position to the desired position. The PID controller then processes this error to generate a control input that tries to minimize it: the proportional (P) part reacts to the size of the error, the integral (I) part reacts to how long the error has been there (accumulated error over time), and the derivative (D) part reacts to how fast the error is changing. The PID controller continuously adjusts the robot's movement based on this feedback, aiming to keep it closely following the target trajectory. The following equations describe this controller mathematically, and Figure \ref{pid} outlines it as a block diagram,

$$e=r-y$$
$$u=k_{P}e+k_{I}\int_{0}^{t}{e\ dt}+k_{D}\dot{e}$$

\end{figure}

\subsubsection{PWM control}

A PWM (Pulse-Width Modulation) is a method used to “convert” digital signal to analog signal by switching on and off the digital output. 3 main concepts of PWM: 

\begin{itemize}
  \item (P) - Pulse, the digital signal 0 (LOW) and 1 (HIGH), 

  \item (W) - Width, the duration of HIGH signal, 

  \item (M) - Modulation, which is the duty cycle which adjusts the duration to control output power. 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sensing}

\subsubsection{Odometry}

Odometry is the process of estimating a robot's change in position (x, y) and orientation ($\theta$) by integrating its measured velocities over time. In wheeled robots, we typically derive those velocities from the wheel encoders' readings i.e. RPM values in our case. 

The process of computing the robot's motion begins with the acquisition of RPM values from the wheels. These RPM values are first converted into angular velocities to represent the rotational speed of each wheel in radians per second. Once the angular velocities ($\omega_{fl}, \omega_{fr}, \omega_{rl}, \omega_{rr}$) are obtained, the forward kinematics equations are applied to determine the robot's linear velocities in the x and y directions, as well as its angular velocity about the z-axis, given the radius of the wheels $r$, and length $l_x$ and width $l_y$ from the 2D centre of the robot

$$ v_x = \frac{r}{4}(\omega_{fl} + \omega_{fr} + \omega_{rl} + \omega_{rr}) $$ 
$$ v_y = \frac{r}{4}(-\omega_{fl} + \omega_{fr} + \omega_{rl} - \omega_{rr}) $$ 
$$ \omega_{z} = \frac{r}{4(l_x+l_y)}(-\omega_{fl} + \omega_{fr} - \omega_{rl} + \omega_{rr}) $$  

Following the computation of the velocities, integration over time is performed to update the robot's position and orientation. This integration yields the robot's displacement along the x and y, and orientation along z, effectively estimating its trajectory over time based on the measured motion data

$$x=\int{v_{x}\,dt}$$
$$y=\int{v_{y}\,dt}$$
$$\theta=\int{\omega_{z}\,dt}$$

\subsubsection{Localisation and Mapping}

Localisation is a fundamental ability required by robots for autonomous navigation, as it involves estimation of the robots pose (position and orientation) in the same frame of reference as its goal. The other component is mapping, which attempts to create a map of the obstacles and areas of interest to the robot, which is crucial to allow for path planning with obstacle avoidance. This gives way to the common problem tackled in robotics known as simultaneous localisation and mapping (SLAM), which attempts to approximate both in a computationally feasible ways. Since all quantities in this problem are stochastic and discrete, the objective is to compute the distribution of the robot state, $x_{t}$, the map of the environment, $m_{t}$, given a series of observations, $o_{1:t}$, and and control inputs, $u_{1:t}$,

$$P(m_{t+1},x_{t+1}|o_{1:t+1},u_{t})$$

Using Bayesian computation, the posterior distributions of each $x_{t}$ and $m_{t}$ can be calculated separately when given the value of the other. Many statistical algorithms exist to tackle this problem, like the Extended Kalman Filter and sequential Monte Carlo method, as well as graph-based methods. Amongst these the sequential Monte Carlo method, also known as the particle filter, is most popular in mobile robots. It models the states and observations as state-space equations given respectively as

$$x_{t}=g(x_{t-1})+W_{t-1}$$
$$o_{t}=h(x_{t})+V_{t}$$

where $W_{t}$ and $V_{t}$ are mutually independent, and the equations are sequentially solved using a number of different Bayesian methods depending on the constraints of the specific system.

\section{Methodology and Implementation}

In the current era of rapid technological advancement, the shift toward autonomous systems has become not only logical but essential. Automation reduces human risk exposure, optimizes operational efficiency, and expands capabilities beyond biological limitations. Tasks involving hazardous materials present significant health and safety concerns for human operators. Exposure to toxic compounds, unstable environments, and unpredictable variables necessitates the development of non-human agents capable of executing such operations with precision and resilience.

This chapter outlines the design methodology and implementation sequence of an autonomous hazardous waste retrieval unit. The system has been engineered for deployment in environments deemed unsafe for prolonged human presence. Operational goals include waste identification, secure collection, and optimized navigation, all executed without direct human intervention.

\subsection{Concept Development and Evaluation}

Our primary novel concept was the manipulator, hence we considered multiple designs, and used a design choice matrix to find the most appropriate one. Our criterion were: the appropriateness and the end-effector to our task and its simplicity; the accuracy that could be achieved in end-effector positions; the static and dynamic (during movement of joints or mobile base) stability of the manipulator; the load it could carry; and finally the complexity of its implementation.

The designs we considered as follow, and their decision matrix scores are shown in the table below,

\begin{itemize}

\item A 2-DOF forklift that would require objects to be placed at some elevation and would function with two linear actuators that would move the hoist horizontally in the direction of the robots movement and vertically up or down.

\item A suction gripper that would be limited to working on flat surfaces, and would have three revolute joints for a full range of motion in 3D space.

\item A crane mechanism with one rotating joint around the z (vertical) axis and one linear actuator to lower or raise the hoist to be able to reach and hold the objects in a safe place.

\item A simple 3-DOF arm with only revolute joints and a two-claw grippers was chosen as a baseline for the rest. It concluded as the best choice and was further developed with a four-bar linkage mechanism to further improve stability by bring the manipulator's centre of the mass lower.

The robot assembly comprises five primary components: the base plate, standoffs, top plate, manipulator arm, and associated electronics. 

\textbf{Step 1: Base Plate Assembly}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Print the base plate using suitable material (e.g., PLA, PETG, or ABS). 

  \item Mount the four motors to the underside of the base plate using four M3 screws per motor (16 M3 screws total). 
  
  \item Attach the mecanum wheels to the motor shafts and secure each wheel with the appropriate fastener. 
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Step 2: Electronics on the Base Plate}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Mount the Raspberry Pi 5 onto the designated holes on the base plate using four M2.5 screws. 

  \item Attach the buck converter to its allocated position using double-sided tape. 
  
  \item Secure the Osoyoo Model Y v2.0 motor driver at the center of the base plate using M3 screws. 
  
  \item Mount the Rplidar opposite the Raspberry Pi. 
  
  \item Install the ESP32-S3 module opposite the buck converter. 
\end{enumerate}

Note: All mounting holes include heat-set inserts for improved screw retention and secure fastening. 

\textbf{Step 3: Wiring and Power Distribution}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Route the motor wires to the ESP32-S3 through the two slots adjacent to the Osoyoo driver. 

  \item Secure the battery beneath the base plate using two zip ties through the same slots. 
  
  \item Connect power lines from the battery to: 
  
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The Osoyoo Model Y v2.0 motor driver. 
    
    \item The buck converter. 
  \end{itemize}
  
  \item Distribute regulated power from the buck converter to the rest of the electronics, including the Raspberry Pi and ESP32-S3. 
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Step 4: Structural Assembly }

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Install four standoffs on the base plate using M3 screws, these standoffs provide vertical clearance for the components and for the lidar to function correctly. 

  \item Attach the top plate to the standoffs and secure temporarily. 
  
  \item Apply super glue to the base of each standoff to bond them to the base plate for structural rigidity. 
  
  \item Once the glue has fully cured, the top plate can be unscrewed to allow access to the internal components without damaging the assembly. 
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Step 5: Top Plate Assembly }

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Mount the base servo underneath the top plate at the specified position. 

  \item Assemble the 3-DOF four-bar linkage manipulator arm on the top plate, interfacing it with the base servo. 
  
  \item Attach the secure payload compartment. 
  
  \item Attach the Oak-D camera on the top plate along with the servo driver. 
  
  \item Complete all wiring from the manipulator servos to the Raspberry Pi or ESP32-S3 as per the control logic.

The LiDAR sensor was responsible for generating 2D laser scans of the surrounding environment. These scans were pre-processed through a custom filtering step implemented in the \code{scan_callback()} function of the Color node. In this filtering process, a forward-facing sector was isolated (defined between -80$^{\circ}$ and +100$^{\circ}$) and invalid or noisy range readings were discarded based on \code{range_min} and \code{range_max} conditions. The filtered data was published on the \code{/scan_filtered} topic, which was then used by the Cartographer SLAM node for map building. Cartographer constructed an occupancy grid map by matching these incoming filtered scans against the existing map. The following listing shows the code for \code{scan_callback}:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def scan_callback(self, msg: LaserScan): 
  scan = msg 
  self.scan_data = msg 
  # Define the front sector in radians 
  front_sector_min = -math.radians(80) # -0.5236 rad 
  front_sector_max = math.radians(100) # 0.5236 rad 
  
  # Calculate the index range corresponding to the front sector 
  # LaserScan angles: angle = scan.angle_min + index * scan.angle_increment 
  start_index = int((front_sector_min - scan.angle_min) / scan.angle_increment) 
  end_index = int((front_sector_max - scan.angle_min) / scan.angle_increment) 
  
  # Ensure indices are within valid bounds 
  start_index = max(0, start_index) 
  end_index = min(len(scan.ranges) - 1, end_index) 
  
  # Extract the ranges within the desired sector and filter valid readings 
  front_ranges = [r for r in scan.ranges[start_index:end_index+1] 
  if scan.range_min <= r <= scan.range_max]:
    if front_ranges: 
      self.closest_distance = min(front_ranges) 
    else: 
      print("No valid range measurements in the front sector.") 
\end{lstlisting}

To further enhance localization robustness, \code{use_online_correlative_scan_matching} was enabled in the Lua configuration, and motion filter parameters such as \code{motion_filter.max_angle_radians} were tuned to suppress noise from minor pose fluctuations. Through the fusion of filtered LiDAR data and IMU measurements, Cartographer achieved more reliable and drift-resistant SLAM performance. While LiDAR provided detailed spatial mapping through scan matching, the IMU contributed to maintaining accurate heading and motion estimation during situations where scan matching alone would have been less reliable.

\subsubsection{Control}

To command the mobile base, we computed the inverse kinematics (IK) of the robot to give us individual wheel velocities. These were then supplied as target velocities to a PID controller per-wheel, to ensure they were followed in stable manner. The IK equations are given as follows

$$\omega_{fl}=\frac{1}{r}(v_{x}-v_{y}-(l_{x}+l_{y})\omega_{z})$$
$$\omega_{fr}=\frac{1}{r}(v_{x}+v_{y}+(l_{x}+l_{y})\omega_{z})$$
$$\omega_{rl}=\frac{1}{r}(v_{x}+v_{y}-(l_{x}+l_{y})\omega_{z})$$
$$\omega_{rr}=\frac{1}{r}(v_{x}-v_{y}+(l_{x}+l_{y})\omega_{z})$$

\subsubsection{Audio feedback}

An audio playback service node was implemented to enable verbal interaction between the robot and its environment. The node, named \code{AudioService}, was developed to enable dynamic sound output on the robot through ROS 2 services. It provides two service endpoints: one to start audio playback and another to stop it. 

This functionality allows the robot to respond to events or user interactions with verbal cues, alerts, or predefined audio messages and it is triggered when the robot detects a human or obstacle directly in front of it. Upon detection, the robot initiates audio playback for a specified duration before executing a maneuverer to navigate around the object. This behaviour allows the robot to provide an audible cue such as a warning or instruction prior to acting, enhancing its interactive and socially aware operation. While this integration of the audio node into the robot's feedback loop demonstrates effective modular design, it is acknowledged that the timing, responsiveness, and contextual awareness of this feature require refinement in the final prototype.

The XL4016 also proved unreliable due to an unspecified fault. Ultimately, the XL4015E1 converter board from Drok was selected. It met all performance requirements, reliably powering the Raspberry Pi, ESP32, servos, and camera. Moreover, it featured an integrated display for monitoring input voltage, helping to prevent over discharge of the LiPo battery which was a failure encountered in earlier versions.

\section{Management and Marketing}

\subsection{Project Management}

The development of Hazmat followed a structured project management approach to ensure timely and effective execution. The project was divided into distinct phases: planning, design, implementation, integration, testing, and final demonstration. Task assignments were made based on individual team members' technical strengths, with regular meetings conducted to monitor progress and resolve arising issues. 

A Gantt chart was created using Microsoft Project to plan and track activities throughout the academic semester. This facilitated effective time management, accountability, and coordination within the team. Weekly meetings provided opportunities for iterative testing and refinement, particularly during critical stages such as sensor integration, SLAM implementation, and mechanical assembly. 

Key milestones of the project included the successful integration of the LiDAR and camera systems, enabling real-time perception of the environment. The team created a custom indoor layout to simulate a hazardous waste handling scenario and designed a complete mechanical structure of the robot, which was then 3D printed and assembled. A teleoperation mode was implemented for manual control and testing, followed by the deployment of autonomous navigation using SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) combined with LiDAR for dynamic map generation. Object detection and colour-based sorting were successfully demonstrated, allowing the robot to identify, pick, and classify hazardous waste materials based on visual input. 

Risk mitigation strategies were implemented to address potential delays in hardware procurement, sensor calibration challenges, and software integration issues.

\subsection{Health, Safety, and Environment}

Hazmat was developed with strict consideration for health, safety, and environmental regulations. All mechanical components were securely assembled, and electrical wiring was insulated and properly routed to avoid short circuits or overheating. 

To ensure safety during operation: 

\begin{itemize}
\item The robot speed was limited to prevent accidental collisions. 

\item Edges on 3D-printed parts were rounded. 

\item The robotic arm was programmed to operate within a controlled range of motion. 

\item Voltage regulators were used to maintain stable power delivery. 
\end{itemize}

From an environmental standpoint, the use of 3D-printed PLA - a biodegradable material - was emphasized. The system's purpose itself addresses environmental sustainability by improving hazardous waste handling, thereby reducing risks of human exposure and environmental contamination. 

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

The Hazmat project represents the outcome of a rigorous engineering effort driven by a shared commitment to innovation, safety, and practical problem-solving. Tasked with developing a robotic solution for hazardous waste handling, our team designed and implemented an autonomous system aimed at minimizing human exposure in high-risk environments.

Throughout the development process, we applied advanced concepts in robotics and autonomous navigation. Notable technical achievements include the successful implementation of SLAM using Cartographer with LiDAR and IMU data, development of a 3-DOF manipulator with a four-bar linkage for efficient and stable object handling, integration of vision-based object recognition and servoing, and deployment of a ROS-based communication stack using micro-ROS for real-time subsystem control. The system also featured feedback-enabled control for improved precision, an interactive audio feedback module, and a simulation environment built in Unity for iterative testing. These accomplishments demonstrate the feasibility of deploying a fully integrated autonomous mobile manipulator in confined and dynamic industrial settings.

Beyond engineering, the project tested our capabilities in project management, market analysis, sustainability, and ethical engineering. We explored real-world deployment scenarios in sectors such as chemical spill response, industrial site cleanup, and post-disaster operations contexts where automation can significantly improve safety and efficiency.

Despite encountering challenges such as power management issues, hardware failure, and mapping drift, we maintained a solution-oriented approach. Each setback contributed to a cycle of learning and improvement that shaped the final system into a cohesive, functioning prototype capable of navigating environments, detecting waste, and performing autonomous collection and disposal.

In conclusion, Hazmat is more than just a demonstration of robotic capability—it reflects how thoughtful design, interdisciplinary teamwork, and technological innovation can come together to address real-world problems. This project lays a strong foundation for future advancements in autonomous hazardous waste management and presents numerous avenues for future work, including the integration of autonomous charging capabilities, enhancement of visual recognition with machine learning, and expansion into outdoor or GPS-based navigation. Further research and refinement will bring this concept closer to industrial deployment and contribute to a safer, more sustainable future.
